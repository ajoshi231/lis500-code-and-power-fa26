Gary Izunwa

Gary Izunwa, co-founder of Tangent and based in the UK, focuses his life's work on helping people from lower-income backgrounds find professional networks they otherwise may not have access to. Izunwa started his career in a leadership program at LinkedIn and later worked at the British Council, where he ran social mobility programs. At Zinc, he met Joe Adams, and the duo went on to found Tangent, which is an employee referral platform that matches job seekers from these communities with employees at tech companies. Based on information provided in Izunwa's POCIT feature, Tangent has raised $1.1 million so far, and in 2024, Forbes named Izunwa to its 30 Under 30 list for social impact.

Hadiyah Mujhid

Hadiyah Mujhid, founder of HBCUvc, focuses her work on expanding access to venture capital and creating opportunities for historically underrepresented communities to participate in the startup ecosystem. A computer science graduate of the University of Maryland Eastern Shore and MBA graduate of Drexel University, Mujhid began her career as a software engineer and later founded Picturely, a computer vision startup. After experiencing firsthand the challenges entrepreneurs face when accessing funding and professional networks, she founded HBCUvc in 2017, a nonprofit that trains students from Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs) in venture capital and connects them with internships, mentorship, and investment opportunities. Through its programs, HBCUvc has helped place 181 fellows in paid venture capital internships, and its alumni participated in investment decisions that directed more than $10 million to Black-founded startups. Mujhid’s work focuses on diversifying the venture capital industry and helping historically excluded communities gain greater access to investment capital and wealth-building opportunities.

Intersectionality: Hadiyah’s work is a great example of intersectionality because HBCUvc focuses on where race, education, and wealth overlap, opening up venture capital networks that usually recruit from elite schools to HBCU students. Lisa Nakamura discussues in “Indigenous Circuits” how Fairchild praised Navajo women’s “innate” skill, which in reality kept them from getting proper credit at the time or a even a fair share of the generational wealth they built with their blood, sweat, and tears; however, HBCUvc flips that script on its head by training Black students to be the investors with a seat at the table, actively making decisions and assuming leadership positions.

Erika Jefferson

Erika Jefferson, President and Founder of Black Women in Science and Engineering (BWISE), focuses her work on closing the leadership gap for Black women in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM). Jefferson earned her bachelor's degree in Chemical Engineering from Louisiana State University and her MBA from Georgia Tech. She began her career in the corporate sector and went on to hold leadership and executive roles at major companies including Amoco, BP, Chevron, and Praxair, working across sales, business development, supply chain, and operations. Drawing on more than two decades of industry experience, Jefferson founded BWISE in 2015 to help underrepresented women advance their careers through mentorship, networking, and professional development. The organization connects Black women in STEM, particularly those in middle and senior management, to share experiences, build professional relationships, and access leadership opportunities. Through BWISE, Jefferson works to increase representation in STEM leadership and support the next generation of women in technical fields.

Intersectionality: Erika’s work is a great example of intersectionality because BWISE was built specifically for Black women, who are often left out when tech diversity efforts focus mostly on white women, something Erica Joy Baker calls “Colorless Diversity” in her piece "#FFFFFF Diversity." BWISE stood out for us because it empowers Black women to be seen as their own selves in the tech industry instead of being forced into just one narrow box or definition of identity.
