A Rock-Paper-Scissors Classifier

Our classifier, trained on Google’s Teachable Machine, recognizes the three main gestures (rock, paper, and scissors) in the classic, old-timey game of rock, paper, scissors.

Try the Model!

  1. Click the "Start Camera" button and allow camera access on your browser.
  2. Hold up rock, paper, or scissors in front of the camera, as shown in the demo video.
  3. Experiment by tilting your hand, bringing it closer to the camera, and/or trying different backgrounds and lighting conditions.
  4. Take a look at the model's interpretation and the probabilities next to it.
  5. Click the "Stop Camera" button when you are done trying out the model.

Project Statement

Intro and Research Question

When starting this project, we had a few different thoughts about what we should do. We landed on a game of rock, paper, scissors because it was the most interactive and seemed fairly easy when it came to difficulty; it is showing three clearly distinct hand moves.

Our research question is: Can an image-based Teachable Machine be given three different hand moves (rock, paper, or scissors) to actively identify what move is thrown?

Data Collected

We collected 300 total images, 100 per class (rock, paper, and scissors), per team member. Ben took 100 images of rock, Brooke took 100 images of scissors, and I (Avani) took 100 images of paper. We made sure to incorporate a level of variation in our training sets by taking pictures of our respective gestures in different angles and lighting conditions to ensure that our model wasn’t simply “memorizing” the answers based on a pre-designated position or background or amount of light in the image, for that matter. The technical term for this is overfitting, Having our training sets evenly split (100/100/100) ensured that our three gestures were being equally and fairly represented, although there are a few additional measures we could have taken to ensure greater representation and mitigate overfitting (see: Connecting to “Unmasking AI”).

How We Trained

To teach our Teachable Machine, we were using different angles and different lighting for each move.

Shortcomings

A few shortcomings we had found when adding the teachable machine to our site was how quickly the results changed due to having an open camera vs just snapping a photo.

Another shortcoming we had, not necessarily with the teachable machine itself, but a recommended change, was having an option to start and stop the camera from working instead of adding additional previews.

Connection to "Unmasking AI"

Unmasking AI, by Dr. Joy Buolamwini, showed us how an AI system works based on who made it and their values/biases. She explains that “AI reflects both the aspirations and the limitations of its makers. AI does not transcend humanity” (Buolamwini, 2023). Since we created our teachable machine, each of us took a move and took 100 pictures. This can cause the system to know only the examples we have given it. To create more diversity inside each move, we could have done an even split between the three of us within our 100/100/100 split so that there is different skin color, hand size, and hand shape for each move to make it more universal and representative of different demographics.

Although it seems like little nuances when trying to train a teachable machine, there is a lot more little factors that need to be thought about. Having examples for each move showing different skin color, child vs adult (hand size), male and female, amputated hands, etc. This would connect to increased intersectionality in our model. It would include individuals that may have multiple overlapping identities that are different than ours which it was trained on. Buolamwini explains how she applied intersectionality and defines it as, “analyzing across multiple axes of identity” (Buolamwini, 2023). So if we were to include images of individuals with different skin colors this does not necessarily mean that our model is equally representative of people with different ages, genders or physical abilities. For example, a child and an adult of a similar skin tone may still have different results using our machine due to the difference in their hand sizes. So just including one factor of demographics does not automatically assume intersectionality as intersectionality is the layered experiences, characteristics, and lifestyles each person holds independently.

In her Gender Shades research, Buolamwini says, “My hunch was that looking at overall accuracy was not enough, even though doing so was the norm” (Buolamwini, 2023). This is a concept we should consider when we are evaluating our own model. Although our training data is evenly distributed across the three moves, this does not mean that our model will perform equally across different demographics. If we were to only evaluate the model’s overall accuracy, then we could overlook groups of people the model performs much worse for.

While having three different people create images to train the teachable machine has more diversity and possibly less bias than just a singular developer, it goes to show that having more people with diverse backgrounds would result in a more unbiased model. It also shows the importance of having multiple people review and look for biases in trained artificial intelligence to reduce the chance for discrimination against minority populations. This should be a more common practice for all trained models, but especially for those that are used in federal instances where we have seen these models fail before. An example referenced within our discussion group is the case of Robert Williams that happened in January 2020 in Detroit, where he was wrongfully accused and arrested due to an inaccurate facial recognition match. Although our rock, paper, scissors model has much lower stakes than a facial recognition system used by law enforcement, the concerns are similar. Buolamwini herself shares a similar belief when she says, “It was one thing if I could not paint a wall with my smile, but if someone was misidentified as a criminal suspect and falsely arrested or worse, then the stakes were unmistakably high” (Buolamwini, 2023). Both of the systems rely on training data to recognize visual patterns to make classifications. So if those systems are not trained and evaluated diversely, they can be incorrect and dangerous in some cases.

Our project also relates to Buolamwini’s concept of the coded gaze, which she describes as “the priorities, preferences, and prejudices of those who have the power to shape technology can propagate harm” (Buolamwini, 2023). Our model was trained on examples that reflected our own physical characteristics and understanding of what each move should look like. We were the ones who decided what our machine would recognize as rock, paper, and scissors based off of our own experiences playing the game. So this raises the question of whether the model would recognize alternative ways of making these hand gestures that we might not have considered/included when we were training it. Because we trained the model on what we think the standards are for each move, this does not mean that they are universal hand gestures.

In doing this project, we have seen firsthand the concepts Buolamwini describes and how difficult it can be to account for unintentional biases and how important it is to consider intersectionality when training and evaluating an AI model.
